# Interview Preparation Guide

## How the candidate prepares (method)

- **Preparation beats improvisation.** An interview gives the employer 30-40 minutes and 5-7 key questions; about 70% of them are predictable. Improvised answers sound vague and read as weaker competence. Every prep doc therefore ships with ready answers to the predictable questions, not with advice to "be yourself".
- **Position: offering expertise, not asking for a job.** "I see you need X; I have done X many times; here is what it did." Candidates interviewing in a second language often freeze under pressure even when they are confident in their first language — the "what I can offer" framing, rehearsed aloud, is the fix.
- **No self-sabotage.** Never "I'm not sure I'm what you're looking for", "I don't have experience in your industry", "I can do anything". Bridge answers lead with real experience.
- **Three-lights check** for any prepared answer: relevant to this vacancy? clear to a non-HR listener in 30 seconds? linked to the employer's problem?
- **UK employers**: competence-focused and understated. Results yes, American-style superlatives no. Warm, polite interviews do not signal a yes.

## The candidate's prepared material — the source of truth

- **Case bank (STAR-L, 60-90 s scripts in the interview language, requirement map):** keep it in one file, e.g. `<NOTES_DIR>/interview-cases.md` (path set in `01-candidate-profile.md`). A useful spread for a People / HR profile: (1) adoption of a performance or grading system, (2) 1:1s and feedback with resistant individual contributors, (3) an org-design case, (4) a crisis / relocation / support programme, (5) building the function from zero plus hiring, (6) scaling through rapid headcount growth, (7) a business case for a new programme, (8) an employee-relations case (frequently asked for in People Partner postings — make sure one exists), (9) a failure with learning.
- **Self-presentation, reason for leaving, five years, why us:** keep it in one file, e.g. `<NOTES_DIR>/self-presentation.md`, with a version per target role family plus a 30-second version.
- Every company prep doc picks 3 cases from the bank, fills the "bridge" line of the self-presentation with this vacancy's problem, and never invents a new story that is not in the bank or the profile.

## Formulas

**Tell me about yourself (1-3 min):** who you are and how long → 2-3 results relevant to this vacancy, with numbers (these are the spotlight: they steer the follow-up questions to your strongest cases) → bridge to their problem + motivation said aloud → "That's the short version, happy to go deeper."

**Where in five years (1-2 min):** the employer checks stability (2-3 years), realism, and whether your growth helps them. Growth by deepening expertise in their domain, realistic horizon, linked to the company. "Five years is hard to predict, but for the next year or two..." is fine.

**Hardest project / failure (STAR-L):** Situation, Task, Action, Result, Learning. Show the scale of the mess and what you did; "nothing was hard" reads as a lie or toy projects. For failures, the Learning step is mandatory: what you do differently now.

## Common Tough Questions

### "Why did you leave [current/last employer]?"
Use the approved script in `01-candidate-profile.md` ("Reason for leaving") verbatim. Keep it forward-looking and never a judgement of former leadership. Rehearse until it sounds calm.

### "You don't have [specific skill/experience]."
Lead with the closest thing the candidate has really done and its result, then one line on how it applies here. No apology, no "you're right, I haven't". Example shape: "The closest I've done is X at [previous employer], where Y happened. Here I'd start by Z."

### "Where do you see yourself in 5 years?"
See the formula above and the scripted answer in the self-presentation file.

### "What's your biggest weakness?"
A real one with the system the candidate built around it (e.g. long maintenance phases drain them, so they design for handover from day one — programmes they built run without them).

### "Why this company specifically?"
Customize per company: their concrete problem (from the posting, news or the interviewer's posts) → the candidate's matching experience → why it interests them. Never generic.

## Before each interview
1. Re-read the CV and letter sent to this company.
2. Research the interviewer on LinkedIn: role, posts, talks, how they write and what they care about. Hiring managers often post what they want to see.
3. Pick 3 cases from the bank; fill the bridge line of the self-presentation.
4. Say the self-presentation aloud three times.

## After each interview
1. If the candidate records calls with a meeting-notes tool and asks for a review, pull that summary, compare the answers to the case bank, and point out where a case could have been opened up more or where an answer ran long.
2. Thank-you note to the recruiter or hiring manager within a day (2-3 sentences), and a LinkedIn connection request to the interviewers — including after a rejection. Interviewers move companies, and an open door can bring candidates back.
3. If declining an offer, decline warmly and keep the door open.

## Questions You Should Ask Interviewers

### About the Role
- "What does a typical week look like in this role?"
- "What would success look like in the first 6 months?"
- "What's the biggest challenge the team is facing right now?"

### About the Team
- "How big is the team, and how do you divide work?"
- "What does the development/project lifecycle look like, from idea to production?"
- "How do you onboard new team members?"

### About Tech & Growth
- "What's your current tech stack for [relevant area]?"
- "Is there room to grow into more architectural or strategic decisions?"
- "How does the team stay current with new tools and methods?"

### About Culture (use these to prevent disappointment)
- "How would you describe the team culture?"
- "What does professional development look like here?"
- "Is there flexibility for remote/hybrid work?"
- "What's the balance between development/new projects and maintenance work?"
- "How would you describe the leadership style in this team?"
- "What do people who thrive here have in common?"

## Phone/Video Interview Tips
- Have the case bank and self-presentation open
- Keep a glass of water nearby
- Smile when speaking (it changes your tone)
- Ask for clarification if a question is vague
- It's OK to take 5 seconds to think before answering
- End with: "Is there anything else you'd like to know about my background?"

## After the Application (Best Practice)

### Follow-Up Etiquette
- **Don't call to "stand out"** or to learn more about the role post-submission - this risks a negative impression
- If the employer specified a timeline, respect it and wait
- If no timeline was given and significant time has passed (2+ weeks), a brief call to ask about status is acceptable
- If you have genuinely new, relevant information to share, a short follow-up is fine

### Thank-You Notes
- When you receive any update (interview invitation, rejection, or status update), send a brief thank-you message
- Express appreciation for their time and the process
- Keep it short (2-3 sentences)

## Roleplay Guidelines
When the user asks for interview practice:
1. Ask which role/company to simulate
2. Start with easy warm-up questions ("Tell me about yourself")
3. Progress to role-specific technical questions
4. Include 1-2 behavioral questions using the competencies from the job posting
5. End with a tough question or curveball
6. After each answer, give brief feedback: what worked, what to sharpen
7. Suggest which STAR example would work best for each question
8. Judge every answer by the three lights (relevance, clarity, link to the employer's problem) and timing (self-presentation 1-3 min, cases 60-90 s). Pauses and restarts are fine; this is the safe place to rehearse.
9. Flag any self-sabotage phrase immediately and give the stronger wording.
